*USDA publishes a pasture rent figure for nearly every farm county in the country. Landowners and renters use it to settle what's fair, and the federal government uses it to set conservation payments. In one Missouri county, the man who fills out the survey says the 2025 number is wrong. The same thin-data problem is why USDA stopped counting chile peppers in two of the four biggest chile states.*
 
---
 
When a landowner in Iron County, Missouri, wonders whether Reginald Kim Harris is paying a fair rent for his pasture, Harris doesn't argue. He brings the county report.
 
> "I can show him the report and he's almost immediately convinced that it's not fabricated in my mind."
 
Harris runs about 100 head of cows on a 500-acre family place in the Ozark hills and rents the pasture on either side of it. The report he brings is the U.S. Department of Agriculture's county estimate of cash rent, published every August. His landlords rarely know what land is going for, he said. The rent "helps them pay their taxes," and they want to be sure it's fair. "They just want to make sure I'm not taking advantage of them because they're not in the know. These landowners that I have probably don't even know that this data exists."
 
For 2025, that report put Iron County pasture at $43.50 an acre. The year before, it was $16.50. The year after, $22.
 
Harris has answered the survey behind that number online for about ten years. Shown the 2025 figure, he didn't hedge.
 
> "Clearly it's not correct. The reason it's not correct is debatable, but there was probably a single report that caused it to be way off base. So the 2025 year should be discarded."
 
He isn't a casual reader of federal statistics. He spent 35 years with USDA's Natural Resources Conservation Service, retiring as Mississippi's state conservation engineer. *(Disclosure: Harris is the author's father.)*
 
A county rent figure is supposed to be the neutral number both sides of a lease can point to. What happened to Iron County's in 2025 shows how much that depends on how many people in a county send the form back.
 
**A note on dates.** A 2026 figure existing before 2026 is over isn't a typo. Cash rent is a price agreed in advance, not a harvest result. USDA's National Agricultural Statistics Service collects it from mid-February through June and publishes county figures in August, so each year's number describes rent already contracted for that year.

## The number carries money
 
The survey isn't optional for the government. The 2008 Farm Bill, amended in 2018 to make it annual, directs NASS to estimate county-average cash rent for cropland and pasture in every county with at least 20,000 acres of the two combined. The stated purpose is program administration at the Farm Service Agency.
 
The clearest use is the Conservation Reserve Program. For cropland, FSA builds each county's CRP payment ceiling from the NASS dryland cash rent estimate, adjusted for soil productivity. For grazing land, Grassland CRP rental rates are based on 75 percent of the NASS pastureland rate. When a county's NASS data isn't sufficient, FSA's state committee can propose an alternative rate, with justification.
 
So a county pasture figure is more than a reference point. It feeds what the federal government pays landowners in that county to keep grassland in grass.
 
Harris never used it that way in his federal career. NRCS writes conservation plans; FSA handles the money.
 
> "No, I didn't use it. NRCS is a technical agency, not financial … FSA takes care of the money … it was kind of an intentional wall between us and FSA. We're sister agencies."
 
His use is the private one: the kitchen-table check. That only works if the number is right. A landowner shown the 2025 report would have seen a county rate more than double the year before.

## Measuring the right thing
 
The survey only counts one kind of rent: cash, by the acre. The questionnaire tells respondents to exclude land rented on shares, per head, or by the animal-unit month, land rented with barns included, and land used free of charge. In cattle country that sounds like a problem. If most pasture moved by the head, the published rate would describe a sliver of the market.
 
In Iron County, it doesn't.
 
> "Virtually all of it, to my knowledge, is per-acre cash. We don't calculate animal units. We just talk about acres. They don't know how many head of cattle I have."
 
What has changed is the paperwork. "We used to operate with a handshake," he said, "but that tends to cause problems when one or the other forgets the agreement." The sticking point is usually timing: landowners want the cattle off before deer or turkey season. Now that's written into a contract.
 
So the survey is measuring the right thing. The question is how many people it's measuring.

## Measuring too few
 
Harris fills out the form online, from his records, usually the day it arrives. He reports the total dollars he paid and lets the system divide by the acres. In ten years, he said, nobody from NASS has called to check an answer.
 
> "If they have that capability, it ain't a problem. You just almost fill it out the same day you get the request and it's a done deal."
 
Since 2021, a county figure hasn't been a simple average of returned forms. NASS now estimates county rates with a statistical model. Its documented inputs include this year's and last year's survey results, the prior year's published figure, a national soil productivity index, and the number of reports received, and the results are tied back to the state total. In a model built that way, the fewer reports a county returns, the more its published rate leans on last year and the state, and the less on what anyone in the county actually paid.
 
NASS publishes a measure of how much to trust each figure, the coefficient of variation, or CV. It estimates how much the number would move if the survey were run again; the lower, the better. Among county pasture figures published for 2025, three out of four had a CV of 11.5 percent or less, according to NASS's own methodology report. Iron County's 2025 figure carried 28.7 percent, the highest of 106 Missouri counties that year. In the years on either side, it was 7.0 and 8.3.
 
Harris reads that the way the statistic intends.
 
> "One report in 2025 threw everything off the scale."
 
Could someone have inflated it on purpose, to raise what landowners could ask? "I would probably agree with that being possible, but probable, no," he said, "because the next year, 2026, this rogue reporter didn't report it … if you wanted to skew the results you would do it multiple years." His guess is simpler. To illustrate, he invented a case: "Somebody hit the wrong button twice and instead of $22 an acre, it was $2,200 an acre."
 
That theory has a complication. A model designed to lean on last year's number when reports are thin should dampen one wild answer, not publish a figure 2.6 times the year before. What produced the 2025 result isn't visible from outside the agency.

Why a county's reporting base gets thin, he doesn't put down to reluctance. "There's only a few of my neighbors that are cattle farmers," he said. Across Missouri, the typical county pasture figure is slightly less precise than in 2021: on the same 95 counties, the median CV rose from 5.0 to 6.0 percent, though not in a straight line. Asked why, he pointed at the herd, not the paperwork.
 
> "I wouldn't hold to the idea that fewer people are responding. I would definitely hold to the fewer operations … It could be a combination of both."

## How chile got uncounted
 
The cash rents survey is required by law. Much of what NASS measures isn't. For chile peppers, the question isn't how few people answer in a county but whether a state gets counted at all.
 
Every five years, after the Census of Agriculture, NASS reviews which crops it will keep estimating in which states. In March 2019 that review dropped Arizona and Texas from the chile program, starting with that year's crop, leaving California and New Mexico. The stated method: rank states by production and value, and keep the ones that account for the largest share, given limited resources. It wasn't a judgment about chile. The same review cut eight states from tomatoes, eight from sweet corn, and every estimating state from lima beans.
 
The main input to that ranking is the Census.
 
| Chile, acres harvested (Census of Agriculture) | 2012 | 2017 | 2022 |
|---|---|---|---|
| New Mexico | 9,577 | 8,313 | 8,484 |
| California | 7,029 | 4,168 | 3,257 |
| Texas | 4,288 | 2,074 | 2,249 |
| Ohio | 698 | 873 | 1,058 |
| Arizona | 1,944 | 1,250 | 386 |
 
In April 2024 the next review restored Texas and added Ohio. Arizona, down 80 percent in a decade, stayed out.
 
That matters for anyone reading USDA's annual chile numbers. Plot them without knowing about 2019 and it looks like an industry collapsing. Some of it was: across the Census decade, California lost 54 percent of its chile acres, and the national total fell at least 26 percent, from at least 31,265 acres in 2012 to 23,122 in 2022. (The 2012 figure is a floor; nine states' numbers were withheld that year.) New Mexico, the state the chile story usually centers on, lost 11 percent. A good part of the drop the annual series seemed to show was the measuring stopping, not the farming.
 
Coverage isn't lost once. It's re-decided every five years against the last Census, and the Census is the form everyone gets.

## A handful of households
 
In Iron County, the pasture rent the federal government publishes, the figure Grassland CRP rates draw on and the number Harris carries to the kitchen table, rests on a small number of people who answer a voluntary survey because it arrives and it's easy.
 
That works until it doesn't. The cash rents survey is statutory and the chile estimates are discretionary, but neither is safe from erosion, and erosion doesn't look like cancellation. It looks like a wider CV, a number that leans harder on last year's, and one year that the people who pay the rent don't believe.
